ABSTRACT
The multi-layer perceptron (MLP) is a highly popular artificial neural network used for classification across
various applications. Swarm intelligence algorithms, such as the grey wolf optimizer, battle royale optimizer,
sine cosine algorithm, and their improved variants, have been widely utilized for training MLP classifiers,
addressing the challenging optimization problem that covers various difficulties in determining the optimal
network parameters to minimize errors while processing datasets. Nevertheless, these prevailing algorithms
frequently encounter problems like stagnation in local optima and inefficient search, leading to a deficiency
in their ability to deliver efficient and accurate solutions. In order to overcome this challenge and provide a
powerful tool for MLP classifier training, the competing leaders grey wolf optimizer (CGWO) is proposed in
this study. CGWO benefits from a novel mechanism of competing leaders that provides a flexible wolf pack
leadership hierarchy to avoid stagnation in local optima and accelerate convergence speed. In addition, a
population diversity-enhanced initiation method is designed to help improve the efficiency of the mechanism
of competing leaders. The performance of CGWO in training MLP classifiers is evaluated in an experiment
comparing CGWO with nine state-of-the-art algorithms on eleven classification datasets representing a wide
range of optimization challenges. CGWO substantially outperforms its competitors regarding optimal fitness,
classification accuracy, and convergence speed; and shows competitive performance in computation time
comparison.

1. Introduction
Heuristics Optimization techniques play a crucial role in addressing
complex problems within the realms of science and engineering. Being
a popular heuristics optimizer, the Swarm intelligence (SI) algorithm
has received significant interest in the research community in recent
years. The SI algorithm is mainly based on inspiration from gregarious creatures or natural phenomena, and its typical and well-known
examples include Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) (Li, Li, Wu, You,
& Zeng, 2022), Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) (Yavuz, Durmuş, & Aydın,
2022), Cuckoo Search Algorithm (CSA) (Lin, Cao and Zhou, 2022), Grey
Wolf Optimizer (GWO) (Mirjalili, Mirjalili, & Lewis, 2014a), Moth–
Flame Optimization (MFO) (Yang, 2023), Squirrel Search Algorithm
(SSA) (Zhu, Zhou, Sun, & Su, 2022), Honey Bee Mating Algorithm
(HBMA) (Jiang, Tsai, Yeh, & Cao, 2017), Firefly Algorithm (FA) (Bacanin et al., 2021), Salp Swarm Algorithm (SSA) (Yang, Shi, Wu,
Qiu, & Wei, 2019), Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA) (Lin, Yu
et al., 2022), Bat Algorithm (BA) (Bajaj, Sangwan, & Abraham, 2022),
Grasshopper Optimization Algorithm (GOA) (Chakravarthy, Chowdary,
Anguera, Mokara, & Satapathy, 2021), Dragonfly Algorithm (DA) (Yang
et al., 2022), Battle Royale Optimizer (BRO) (Rahkar Farshi, 2021),

Water Cycle Algorithm (WCA) (Taib & Bahreininejad, 2021), and Sine
Cosine Algorithm (SCA) (Yang et al., 2023). Due to their characteristics
of simplicity, convenience, and robustness, these SI algorithms have
been widely admitted in the optimization field and used to solve various optimization problems in the real world (Chakravarthy, Chowdary,
Dib, & Anguera, 2022; Jiang et al., 2017). Notably, SI algorithm has
also emerged as a valuable asset in enhancing machine learning tools
and applications. SI algorithm aids machine learning methods in several aspects, such as feature selection (Bacanin, Budimirovic et al.,
2022; Malakar, Ghosh, Bhowmik, Sarkar, & Nasipuri, 2020), model
training (Bacanin, Bezdan, Al-Turjman and Rashid, 2022; Yang, 2023),
and data clustering (Taib & Bahreininejad, 2021), leading to enhanced
accuracy and efficiency. This symbiotic relationship between SI algorithm and machine learning has paved the way for remarkable
advancements across various domains, empowering the development
of cutting-edge applications ranging from image recognition to natural
language processing.

Training for the network parameters of an MLP is accomplished
according to evolving the weights and biases to minimize the total
network error, which can be considered as an optimization problem.
The training process of finding the best combination of the network parameters based on the training samples and the corresponding desired
outputs significantly affects the performance of the MLP in solving the
target problems.
The problem of training MLPs is very challenging and usually
has complex characteristics in the search space with many local solutions (Faris, Aljarah, & Mirjalili, 2016). Swarm intelligence algorithms proved efficient in finding a global solution when the search
space is challenging. Over past recent years, the well-regarded SI

In this study, to provide an efficient tool for training MLP classifiers
for various applications in health care, engineering, agriculture, etc., an
enhancement of GWO named competing leaders grey wolf optimizer
(CGWO) is proposed. And the CGWO algorithm has three key strengths
to improve the efficiency of training MLP classifiers as follows:
(1) A mechanism of competing leaders that features a flexible
leadership hierarchy of the wolf pack is proposed to avoid stagnation
in local optima and accelerate the convergence speed of the algorithm.
(2) A novel population initialization method based on both chaotic
initialization and random initialization enhances the diversity of the
population, thus, provides a well-distributed initial population to ensure a more efficient subsequential search.
(3) The CGWO has an acceptable computational efficiency and does
not introduce any additional parameters needed to be tuned; thus, it is
convenient to be used as the general optimization tool for training MLP
classifiers.
The experiment on UCI (University of California) classification
datasets that cover the field of healthcare, agriculture, communication,
and finance has shown that, in terms of search accuracy, stability, and
computational efficiency in training different MLPs with a wide range
of problem dimensions, the proposed CGWO algorithm outperforms
all the nine competitor algorithms, including the original GWO, five
state-of-the-art GWO improved techniques, and three other algorithms
reported as the powerful MLPs trainers.

the state-of-the-art techniques, combined with its advantage of not
requiring any parameter configuration, enables researchers to allocate

Based on the search agent representation and the fitness function
definition, the MLP training by swarm intelligence algorithms is formulated as a typical optimization problem that needs to minimize the
average cross-entropy error generated by the network on all training
samples as the inputs. The process of training an MLP using swarm
intelligence algorithms is illustrated in Fig. 2. Firstly, the values of the
weights and biases of an MLP are set with each search agent of a swarm
intelligence algorithm. Then the average cross-entropy error generated
by this specified MLP on all training samples is set as the fitness of the
corresponding search agent. The swarm intelligence algorithms update
the search agents in iterations and finally reach an optimum that has
the least average cross-entropy error on all training samples.
Different target datasets under the process by the MLPs always have
distinct properties, including the problem dimensions and the shape
of the fitness landscape. And thus, they pose different challenges to
the optimization algorithm. Although the No-Free-Lunch theorem (Mirjalili, Mirjalili, & Lewis, 2014b) illustrates that no ideal algorithm can
provide good solutions for training an MLP for all available datasets,
the demand of users for a single tool capable of handling the training
tasks of MLPs applied in different datasets with diverse characteristics
is reasonable. That is because such desired optimization tools avoid the
trial-and-error procedure in selecting a proper algorithm for a specified
MLP training. Nevertheless, the existing SI techniques for MLP classifier
training have some noticeable drawbacks concerning two issues: (1)
Some existing SI-based MLP classifier trainers are designed for specified
target applications and cannot be applied to other applications. Such
techniques are mostly incapable of meeting the demand for general
optimization tools of MLP classifiers. (2) The performance of most
existing SI algorithms is insufficient as the general optimization tools
for MLP classifiers training, and thus they cannot provide acceptable
solutions in many situations. Therefore, to avoid the inefficient trialand-error method of selecting an algorithm for handling each specified
MLP classifier training problem, it is valuable to provide a general
efficient optimization tool for MLPs to handle various classification
datasets with a wide range of characteristics.

2.1. The concepts of GWO
In GWO, the search population consists of two types of grey wolves:
wolf leaders (dominant wolves) and follower wolves. In the group of
wolf leaders, there are three members: the alpha (𝛼) wolf, representing
the best solution found so far in the search space; the beta (𝛽) wolf,
representing the second-best solution; and the delta (𝛿) wolf, representing the third-best solution. The rest of the population are considered
as followers, namely omega (𝜔) wolves. The behavior of the social
hunting mechanism of the grey wolf group in GWO includes encircling
and hunting. The 𝛼, 𝛽, and 𝛿 are supposed to have better knowledge
about the potential location of prey. Therefore, mathematically, the
behavior of a grey wolf chasing and encircling the prey is implemented
by following the wolf leaders. The corresponding model of each grey
wolf in the encircling phase is given in Eqs. (6) and (7) as follows:

2.2. GWO improved variants
Due to its robustness and easy implementation, GWO has been applied to various application scenarios, such as mechanical engineering
design (Banaie-Dezfouli, Nadimi-Shahraki, & Beheshti, 2021), medical
diagnosis (Li et al., 2017), power system control (Guha, Roy, & Banerjee, 2016), and photovoltaic solar cell model estimate (AlShabi, Ghenai,
Bettayeb, Ahmad, & El Haj Assad, 2021). However, when solving
some complex optimization problems, such as large-scale problems or
multi-modal problems, GWO lacks sufficient global convergence ability
and suffers from poor search efficiency, which results in local optima
trapping and slow convergence speed (Nadimi-Shahraki et al., 2021;
Wang, Zhu et al., 2022). Hence, to overcome these weaknesses, various
improved versions of GWO have been proposed in recent years. The
main approaches focus on improving GWO from three different aspects,
including (1) changing the behavior of the grey wolves for hunting, (2)
changing the social leadership hierarchy of the grey wolf group, and
(3) integrating the search patterns from other algorithms into GWO.
In the first category approaches, the behavior of the grey wolf group
is modified by adding or removing some terms of the position update
equations or by changing the generating method of the corresponding
terms. For example, to improve the exploration ability, the grey wolf
is updated with one of the five best solutions as the search center in
the Representative-based GWO (R-GWO) (Banaie-Dezfouli et al., 2021).
Instead of following the original linear decrease scheme, the exploration/exploitation parameter, 𝑎, is adaptively tuned according to the
significance of the change of the search agents’ average fitness improvement in the Adaptive GWO (AGWO) (Meidani, Hemmasian, Mirjalili,
& Barati Farimani, 2022). Moreover, the stop mechanism of AGWO is
also based on the significance of fitness improvement in iteration; the
evolution process is stopped when the fitness improvements become
negligible. In Mutation-Driven Modified GWO (MDM-GWO) (Singh &
Bansal, 2022), when determining the final position of each grey wolf,
the three components obtained in the direction of each wolf leader

The improved versions of GWO in the second category focus on
improving the search efficiency of GWO by changing the social leadership hierarchy of the wolf pack, in other words, the roles of the
grey wolves for hunting. In Incremental GWO (IGWO) (Seyyedabbasi
& Kiani, 2021) and Expanded GWO (ExGWO) (Seyyedabbasi & Kiani,
2021), by using the position update equations of GWO, each grey wolf
is updated with the wolf leader(s) defined in the original GWO and
the wolves previously updated in the same iteration. In other words,
for a certain wolf, besides 𝛼, 𝛽 and 𝛿, the wolves previously updated
in the same iteration are also regarded as the wolf leaders. Therefore,
the number of wolf leaders for each grey wolf is different. The main
difference between IGWO and ExGWO is that the minimum number of wolf leader(s) involved in the position update for each search
agent is 1 for IGWO and 3 for ExGWO. In Group-based SynchronousAsynchronous GWO (SA-GWO) (Rodríguez et al., 2021), to achieve a
better balance between exploration and exploitation, the population
is divided into three subgroups and each subgroup is individually
processed with a certain probability of interchange of elements among
the subgroups. Similarly, to achieve better robustness and faster convergence speed, the Multi-Group GWO (MG-GWO) (AlShabi et al., 2021)
divides the grey wolves into multiple subgroups and updates each
grey wolf with the wolf leaders in each subgroup. In sub-population
improved GWO with Gaussian mutation and Levy flight (SPGWO) (Yu,
Duan and Cai, 2023), the population is equally divided into superior
and inferior two sub-populations by fitness value and updated with
different strategies based on the fitness values comparison between the
two sub-populations.
The GWO variants in the third category can be classified as hybrid
algorithms. Two novel search strategies are designed by combining
the position update equations of GWO and PSO and used to update
the search agents alternatively in Hybrid Particle Swarm and GWO
(HGWOP) (Zhang et al., 2021). In Sine Cosine GWO (SC-GWO) (Gupta,
Deep, Moayedi, Foong, & Assad, 2021), a modified sine cosine algorithm and GWO are run in sequence to establish a comparatively better
synergy between the exploration and exploitation of the algorithm.
In PSOGWO (Adnan et al., 2021), to improve the capability of PSO,
which acts as the main optimization algorithm, of escaping from local optima, the best solutions generated by the assistance algorithm,
GWO, are used to substitute some particles of PSO. For balancing the
local and global search and maintaining diversity, the Improved GWO
(IGWO) (Nadimi-Shahraki et al., 2021) introduces a differential approach, namely dimension learning-based hunting (DLH), to construct
a neighborhood for each wolf in which the neighboring information

3. The proposed CGWO algorithm
For processing complex optimization problems like MLP training,
the crucial characteristic of the metaheuristic algorithm is the capability of exploring unknown areas of the search space to escape the
local optima and exploiting the promising areas confirmed so far to
approximate the optimal results at a reasonable rate.
In the proposed CGWO algorithm, the mechanism of competing
leaders is proposed and used to alleviate the problem of insufficiency
in global and local search abilities and thus improve the ability to
approximate the global optimum and accelerate the convergence speed.
Moreover, a new population initiation method is also designed to
improve the initial population diversity, ensuring a more efficient
subsequential search of the algorithm. Further, CGWO inherits the superiority of the easy implementation and low computational complexity
from GWO, does not require tuning any additional parameters and has
an acceptable computational efficiency for practical applications.
In what follows, the motivation and the implementation of the
mechanism of competing leaders are presented first, then the procedure of CGWO will be introduced in the second subsection, and the
computational complexity of CGWO will be discussed in the third
subsection.
3.1. The mechanism of competing leaders
3.1.1. Motivation
In GWO, the strategy of multiple search centers brought by the
three leaders is designed to achieve exploration for each search agent
attracted by the three leaders from different directions assisted by the
random terms in the position update equations. The wolf in GWO is
obliged to follow the leaders and search in different areas discovered
so far with the potential to find the prey (optimum). The hunting
process is terminated when the wolf leaders confirm the position of
the prey that has stopped moving. The exploration and exploitation
are controlled mathematically with the value of 𝑎 in Eq. (10). The
search behaviors of the wolves in the first half of the iterations focus
on exploration, whereas the other half of the iterations are dedicated
to the exploitation (Emary, Zawbaa, & Grosan, 2017).
The exploration capability of SI algorithms allows them to systematically venture into unexplored regions of the search space, uncovering
potentially more optimal or promising solutions than those already
explored. In the optimization problem of MLP training, it is stated in
the literature that exploration is crucial for approximating a satisfactory
result (Mirjalili, 2015). More measures, such as having more random
and abrupt search steps, should be taken to enhance the diversity of
the population when processing complex datasets with MLPs (Mirjalili
et al., 2014b). However, the search pattern employed by GWO may
potentially undermine the effectiveness of exploration, even during the
initial half of iterations. If each of the three wolf leaders is close to
one another, the multiple search centers practically tend to degenerate
into a singular search center. The scheme of a single search center reduces opportunities for search individuals to learn from other excellent
solutions, resulting in a rapid loss of population diversity of swarm
intelligence algorithms, as reported in the literature (Wang, Hao, Chen,
Wang, & Jiang, 2021). Despite the utilization of a relatively large value
for parameter 𝑎 at this stage, which promotes extensive exploration
around the search center as indicated by Eqs. (7), (9), and (10), the population’s diversity diminishes rapidly due to the reliance on a singular
search center in the search pattern. Especially when the three wolf leaders get stuck in a local optimum simultaneously, inefficient search is
inevitable, and the search agents are less likely to escape from the local
5

optimum. As an example, under the experimental analysis, it is founded
that the three-leaders scheme reduces the diversity of the population
and causes GWO to fall into the local optimum and, finally, premature
convergence (Nadimi-Shahraki et al., 2021). On the other hand, in each
iteration, the three wolf leaders of GWO are not updated until all search
agents are repositioned and evaluated, and this strategy weakens the
exploitation search around promising areas and results in slow convergence speed. In a research case, the slow convergence speed of GWO
is demonstrated in an experiment (Wang, Zhu et al., 2022). Due to the
two aspects of drawbacks in the search pattern, when solving complex
optimization problems such as large-scale problems (Zhang et al., 2020)
or multi-modal problems (Wang, Liu et al., 2022), GWO lacks the power
to reach a globally optimal solution or has a slow convergence speed, or
even suffers from both aspects of poor performance. For example, the
experiment carried on the well-known nineteen benchmark functions
with one hundred dimensions illustrates the poor performance of GWO
in large-scale optimization (Zhang et al., 2020). Regarding the multimodal problems optimization, in the experiment conducted on the
test set consisting of fifteen widely used multimodal numerical test
functions, the performance of GWO is unsatisfactory (Wang, Liu et al.,
2022).
To tackle this problem, a mechanism of competing leaders for the
wolf group is proposed and improves the GWO in two manners: (1)
it introduces the wolf leaders with an extensive membership in the
initial stage, whereas the number of leaders decreases over the course
of competition; (2) it provides a compact strategy for identifying the
newly found good solutions as the wolf leaders.

3.1.3. The compact wolf leaders update strategy
The second technique of the mechanism of competing leaders,
namely the compact wolf leaders update strategy (CWLU), provides a
greedy scheme to guarantee the leader wolves are the best solutions
found so far when the position of each wolf is updated.
According to the mechanism of hunting behaviors of the wolves
in GWO, wolf leaders can be regarded as pins or flags marked by
members of the wolf population in searching for the optima. Although
the diversity of the population is enhanced by the mechanism of competing leaders, the large leader group scheme smooths the influence of
every single leader acting on the population. As a result, the hunting
behaviors of the wolves may not be sensitive enough when a new
promise area is found and marked by one of the leaders. And if some
members of the leaders are not good enough, an inefficient search is
inevitable.
The CWLU strategy can shorten the waiting period required for
better leaders to influence the search behaviors of the wolves, and
it is beneficial for guiding the wolves to perform the search more
efficiently. Therefore, the CWLU strategy is an effective complementary method for the scheme of extensive leaders, helping maintaining
population diversity without weakening the high-efficiency search of
the population. The two strategies of the mechanism of competing
leaders cooperate to achieve the balance between exploitation and
exploration of the algorithm, in which the well-distributed population
is emphasized in earlier iterations, and the population gradually tilts
towards convergence and later achieves rapid convergence when the
evolution progresses.

To evaluate the proposed CGWO technique, we compare the results
obtained by CGWO and other competing algorithms by quantifying
the objective function value (fitness value) and classification accuracy.
The objective function value, being the metric that most intuitively
reflects the algorithm’s optimization capability, is thus employed as
the primary performance metric for evaluating experimental results.
In addition, classification accuracy is also important for analyzing the
results of MLP training. But considering that classification accuracy
can mislead the results when data classes are imbalanced, it is used
as an auxiliary metric to evaluate the algorithm’s performance from
another perspective. The classification accuracy includes two items:
classification accuracy with training samples (training classification accuracy) and classification accuracy with test samples (test classification
accuracy). The training and the test classification accuracies refer to
the correct rate of classification decisions in the training and test sets,
respectively. Moreover, to illustrate the computation efficiency of the
competing algorithms, the CPU computation time and the convergence
speed are investigated.

for each competing algorithm on each dataset were calculated based

order to ensure reliable and confidential comparisons, the statistical
analysis described in Refs. Yang, Wu, and Min (2015) was employed.
The overall framework of the statistical analysis is outlined in Fig. 6.
Firstly, the distribution of the results is assessed by conducting the
Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to determine whether they are following a
normal (Gaussian) distribution or not. If the results do not adhere to
a normal distribution, the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test is performed to compare the result of each algorithm. Conversely, if the
results do follow a normal distribution, the homogeneity of variances
is checked using the Levene test. The confidence of comparisons is
verified by conducting a Welch test if the samples have different
variances; otherwise, an ANOVA test is carried out to accomplish this
task. The statistical analysis is performed at a confidence level of 95%.

4.1.2. The compared algorithms
The original GWO (Mirjalili, 2015), five improved versions of GWO,
including IGWO(M) (Nadimi-Shahraki et al., 2021), AGWO (Meng
et al., 2021), RSMGWO (Zhang et al., 2020), ExGWO (Seyyedabbasi
& Kiani, 2021), and IGWO(A) (Seyyedabbasi & Kiani, 2021), together
with three other remarkable optimization algorithms, including BRO
(battle royale optimizer) (Agahian & Akan, 2022), MSCA(modified
sine cosine algorithm) (Gupta et al., 2020), and RDE-OP (regionbased differential evolution algorithm incorporation opposition-based
learning) (Mousavirad, Schaefer, Korovin, & Oliva, 2021) were adopted
as the algorithms for comparison with the proposed CGWO.
When choosing the compared algorithms, besides the original GWO,
the outstanding GWO improved versions are considered first. The
IGWO(M) algorithm is regarded as one of the most promising and
highly cited improved versions of GWO; it shows very competitive
optimization ability in solving engineering problems over the wellregarded algorithms such as Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA),
Exploration-Enhanced Grey Wolf Optimizer (EEGWO), and Henry Gas
Solubility Optimization (HGSO). Moreover, the IGWO(A) and ExGWO
algorithms also belong to those highly referenced GWO variants and
have been selected as the comparison algorithms by many researchers.
Since two of the compared algorithms have the same abbreviations of
IGWO, we distinguish them using the first letter of the first author’s
first name in the parentheses after the algorithms’ names.
Then, those algorithms designed for training MLP are taken into
account. AGWO is an improved version of GWO designed to solve
MLP optimization problems. It has been verified as a superior tool
to train MLP classifiers compared with some well-known and state-ofthe-art algorithms, including PSO, GA, Biogeography-based Optimizer
(BBO) and Butterfly Optimization Algorithm (BOA). RSMGWO is another GWO variant used to train MLPs and shows good performances
over many other algorithms, including BBO, Ant Colony Optimization
(ACO), Population-based Incremental Learning (PBIL), etc.
Further, the three other algorithms, BRO, MSCA, and RDE-OP,
are also adopted for comparison in the experiment because these
algorithms show excellent performance in the optimization for MLP
training.

4.1.3. Methodology
The 𝑘-fold cross-validation is employed for evaluation, in which the
dataset is randomly partitioned into 𝑘 folds; one-fold is used to test the
network, whereas the remaining 𝑘-1 folds are used as the training sets.
Each of the 𝑘 folds is selected once for the testing, and the process of
training and testing is repeated 𝑘 times. In this study, 𝑘 is set to 5, and
thus 5-fold cross-validation (5CV) is performed.
As the stochastic optimization techniques, the competing algorithms
in the simulation experiment can lead to different results in different
independent runs. To generate meaningful statistical results, all the
empirical results are achieved by taking the average of 10 independent
runs for each dataset. In each run, the average result of the 5-fold
cross-validation is recorded.
For a fair comparison, all competing algorithms were implemented
in MATLAB 2015a and performed on a personal computer with a
3.40 GHz CPU and 8 GB RAM under the Windows 7 operating system.

5. Conclusion
In this study, a novel competing leaders grey wolf optimizer
(CGWO) is proposed and used for training the MLP classifier to get
the optimal value of network parameters. Under comparison with nine
other state-of-the-art algorithms, the proposed CGWO-MLP trainer is

The superiority of CGWO to approximate the global optima accurately, stably, and fastly over the other competing algorithms can be

Therefore, it can be summed up that the proposed CGWO is an efficient
and flexible optimization tool for training the MLP classifiers in various
application fields.
The future work includes three aspects: (1) Exploring the efficiency
of CGWO in accurately defining both the structure and connection
weights and biases of MLPs would be an interesting area of investigation; (2) Regarding the further enhancement of CGWO, on the one
hand, the CGWO can be improved following the ideas of a more delicate
structure of grey wolves’ leadership hierarchy; on the other hand, to

attributed to the mechanism of competing leaders that can avoid stagnation in local optima and accelerate convergence speed. In addition,

the search efficiency of the population. In terms of classification accuracies on both the training and test datasets, CGWO also yields promising
results and outperforms all the competitors. Regarding the computation
time to generate the final solution in the experiment, CGWO is in the
middle of all algorithms and slower than the original GWO and some
other competing algorithms with simpler implementations. This can
be regarded as a drawback that limits the application of CGWO in
scenarios where extremely fast processing is required. Nevertheless, the
